Investment – Dividends

"The habit of saving is itself an education; it fosters every virtue, teaches self-denial, cultivates the sense of order, trains to forethought, and so broadens the mind." - T.T. Munger

Financial Advice

Financial advice consists of guidance or recommendations intended to help individuals make informed financial decisions based on their circumstances, goals, and needs. It may cover financial planning, investments, savings, debt management, and financial products. Certain financial activities require specific registration, licensing, or regulatory authorization.

Financial and Investment Products

Financial and investment products include mutual funds, shares, bonds, and exchange-traded funds (ETFs). Some activities involving these products require additional licensing or registration. Requirements depend on the product, the activity being performed, and the professional's role.

Financial Advisor

  • A financial advisor provides financial and investment recommendations within the scope of their qualifications and authorization.
  • Advisors may specialize in areas such as investments, insurance, or other financial services.
  • In Ontario, the title Financial Advisor is protected under the Financial Professionals Title Protection Act, 2019.
  • Individuals using the title must hold an approved credential from a credentialing body approved by the Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario (FSRA) and remain in good standing.
  • The right to use the title does not automatically authorize every regulated financial activity. Dealing in securities or selling insurance may require separate registration or licensing.

Financial Planner

A financial planner takes a broader view of an individual's financial circumstances and short- and long-term goals.

Planning may cover:

  • Retirement planning
  • Investment planning
  • Tax planning
  • Estate planning
  • Insurance and risk management
  • Cash-flow and debt management

What Should Consumers Check?

  • Credentials
  • Registration or licensing, where applicable
  • The firm, dealer, or financial institution with which the professional is associated
  • Good-standing status
  • The applicable regulator or credentialing organization

Shares

We selected shares from several industries: Communication, Technology, Resources and Energy, Real Estate, Management, Asset Management, and Conglomerates.

Each industry is an important part in the economy and society. Communication is essential for connecting people and businesses, while Technology drives innovation and progress. Resources and Energy provide the materials and power needed for everyday life and economic development. Real Estate supports our fundamental need for shelter and contributes to infrastructure and transportation through the development of properties and related facilities. Management helps organizations operate efficiently and solve problems, and Investments and Asset Management focuses on managing financial resources, capital, and liquidity. Alas, conglomerates allow us to diversify our investments across different business sectors.

As we continue learning, we aim to broaden our selection of shares and explore additional industries. Our initial share selections were based primarily on dividend yield, as a higher dividend yield can provide greater income relative to the share price. However, we recognize the limitations of this strategy. Dividends are not guaranteed, and companies that distribute a large portion of their earnings as dividends may have less capital available for reinvestment and future growth.

Despite these limitations, we selected publicly traded companies that are well-established and widely recognized, and most of these companies are supported by extensive historical data. These characteristics allow us to study their past performance, dividend histories, and financial trends to better understand their potential as investments. Nevertheless, a strong track record does not guarantee future returns, so we will continue to evaluate our selections as our knowledge and investment experience develops.

Analysis and Performance Evaluation

  • Estimated annual dividend income: $14.58.
  • Portfolio dividend yield: 2.37%, based on the initial portfolio cost of $615.15. Using the reported fair value of $601.11, the estimated current dividend yield is approximately 2.43%.
  • Purchase date: August 4, 2026.
  • Fourth-quarter dividend income: Approximately $3.65, based on our dividend calculations.
  • Unrealized fair-value loss: $14.04.
  • Total comprehensive loss: $10.39, calculated by subtracting the unrealized loss of $14.04 from dividend income of $3.65.

Dividend Yield

Six investments in our dividend analysis have reported dividend yields above 2%: Rogers, TELUS, Bell, Vermilion, BlackRock, and the Berkshire Hathaway investment. We considered selling investments with dividend yields below 2% and reallocating the proceeds to higher-yielding investments to increase our annual dividend income. Reallocating funds to investments with higher sustainable dividend yields could increase annual dividend income.

However, companies with lower dividend yields may reinvest earnings to generate stronger long-term growth and increase capital gains. Selling investments in different industries based on dividend yield could reduce diversification. High-yield investments may carry greater financial risk, and their dividends may be reduced or suspended. Also, selling investments may involve transaction costs and tax consequences, depending on the account and circumstances.

Comparing TELUS and Berkshire Hathaway-Linked Investments

The reported dividend yield of the Berkshire Hathaway investment is higher than that of TELUS. However, the two investments have different prices and business models. According to our fair-value table, TELUS declined from $13.37 to $11.34, representing a decrease of $2.03 per share. This is an unrealized loss while the investment remains unsold.

Selling the investments now would record our investment at an irredeemable loss. We also considered concentrating the portfolio in TELUS to increase dividend income. However, holding only one company would expose the portfolio to substantial company-specific and industry risks. A diversified portfolio may provide a better balance between income generation and risk management.

Fair-Value Changes and Investment Losses

Rogers Communications experienced the largest reported dollar decline in our fair-value table, with a loss of $7.64. These declines represent unrealized losses because the investments have not been sold.
If we sell an investment below its purchase cost, the loss becomes realized for investment performance purposes. Selling may prevent us from benefiting from a subsequent recovery. Retaining an investment also does not guarantee that its value will recover. Our decisions should therefore depend on the company's strategy and focus.

Alternative Investment Strategies

We explored several strategies that could influence the portfolio's income and risk profile.

  • Purchase shares with dividend yields above 2%: This may increase income, provided the dividends are sustainable and the investments are reasonably valued. This could raise dividend income, but may also increase risks.
  • Share prices and valuation: Lower share prices may allow investors to purchase more shares with the same amount of capital, but a low share price does not automatically indicate good value.
  • Fixed-income investments: Low risk bonds provide less interest income. Also, bond prices can fluctuate influenced by interest rate.
  • Bond ETFs such as XGB: A bond ETF can provide exposure to a portfolio of government bonds. Although it is not a guaranteed annuity, XGB does not have a fixed maturity date. The fund continually invests in newly issued bonds as the older portfolio bonds mature. However, share prices can fluctuate due to interest-rate changes.
  • International investments: Investments linked to U.S. companies may provide geographic diversification and access to different business opportunities. However, currency exposure depends on the investment structure. Currency-hedged products can reduce exchange-rate effects but may involve costs and tracking differences.

Portfolio Allocation and Dividend Contributions

We also calculated the percentage of the portfolio allocated to each investment and the percentage of total annual dividend income contributed by each holding. Our analysis identified Orbit Garant Drilling as representing approximately 0.21% of the portfolio while contributing 0% dividend income. We have retained this investment because we are interested in its potential for capital appreciation and its exposure to the resource and drilling-services industry.

External Factors Affecting Share Prices

  • Complementary products and services: Growth in AI, cloud computing, data centres, and digital services can increase demand for related products and infrastructure.
  • Competition and substitutes: Competition can influence pricing, market share, customer retention, and profitability.
  • Government policy and regulation: Changes in taxation, interest rates, environmental standards, and industry regulations can affect costs and investment returns.
  • Consumer demand and preferences: Changes in customer needs may create new opportunities or challenges.
  • Technological developments: AI, automation, 5G, and cybersecurity can improve productivity and generate new revenue, but they may require substantial investment.
  • Personal investment preferences: Investors may consider dividend income, Canadian ownership, environmental responsibility, risk tolerance, and financial objectives.

Risk and Diversification

  • Market risk: Losses caused by broad stock market declines or changes in investor sentiment.
  • Company-specific risk: Problems such as declining sales, management difficulties, excessive debt, or weaker earnings.
  • Dividend-reduction risk: The possibility that a company reduces or suspends its dividend.
  • Interest-rate risk: Changes in interest rates that affect borrowing costs, bond prices, and stock valuations.
  • Industry risk: Developments that negatively affect several companies in the same sector.
  • Currency risk: Exchange-rate changes that affect foreign investments and income converted into Canadian dollars.
  • Economic risk: Inflation, recessions, unemployment, and slower economic growth.
  • Regulatory risk: Changes in laws, taxes, or government policies that increase costs or restrict business activities.
  • Liquidity risk: The possibility that an investment cannot be sold quickly at a reasonable price.

To manage these risks, we should diversify across industries and geographic markets, evaluate financial quality, monitor dividend sustainability, compare market prices with estimated intrinsic values, and ensure that our portfolio remains consistent with our financial objectives and risk tolerance.

Other Stock Valuation Methods

Dividend yield is only one measure of investment performance. We can also use the following methods:

  • Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio: Compares a company's share price with its earnings per share to evaluate its valuation relative to earnings.
  • Price-to-Book (P/B) Ratio: Compares market value with accounting net assets and can be useful for certain financial institutions and asset-intensive businesses.
  • Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM): Estimates the required return on equity based on the risk-free rate, systematic risk, and expected market risk premium.
  • Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) Analysis: Estimates intrinsic value by discounting projected future cash flows to their present value.

Transaction Costs and Bookkeeping

We did not incur commission fees when purchasing the stocks included in our portfolio. This reduced our direct transaction expenses. However, commission-free trading does not eliminate all investment costs. Currency conversion charges, fund management fees, bid-ask spreads, and other expenses may still affect returns.

Conclusion: Economic Conditions and the Portfolio

Since we started our research, we have observed that Canadian share prices have been declining. The following economic factors provide possible explanations for changes in share prices and portfolio performance. However, they should be treated as factors to investigate rather than established causes without supporting evidence.

Trade Policy and Cross-Border Relations

Tariffs and other trade measures can increase the cost of imported goods, disrupt supply chains, and raise production costs for Canadian businesses that rely on imported materials, equipment, and components. At the same time, trade restrictions may encourage consumers and businesses to consider domestic suppliers, Canadian-made products, and new import relationships.
Trade uncertainty may also influence investment decisions, business expansion, and capital allocation. Recently, there has been a decline in Canadian share prices, but we do not claim that Canadians are withdrawing investments or selling shares to finance businesses or infrastructure projects. This should be supported by reliable evidence before being used to explain market performance.

Life Insurance and Investment Flows

Life insurance companies collect premiums and manage pooled funds to meet future policy holder obligations. The pooled funds are then invested in equities, bonds, real estate, infrastructure, and other assets, with the expectation that the returns will help to provide payments to policyholders.
Their investment decisions can therefore contribute to demand for financial assets, including shares, and increase their prices. Changes in insurance products, consumer preferences, demographics, and the availability of lower-cost insurance may reduce the pooled funds available for investment. However, more affordable insurance does not necessarily reduce investments in equities.

Interest Rates and Investment Decisions

Interest rates influence borrowing costs, bond yields, company valuations, and the relative attractiveness of different investments. When yields on lower-risk fixed-income investments rise, some investors may shift funds away from riskier shares toward bonds or savings products.

Portfolio Implications

Despite economic uncertainty, households and businesses continue to require essential goods and services, including communications, technology, energy, infrastructure, housing, resources, and professional services.
These ongoing needs can support business activity and create opportunities for companies operating in different industries. Our portfolio's exposure to several industries provides some diversification, while our emphasis on dividend income reflects our objective of generating a potential source of passive income.
Nevertheless, dividend payments are not guaranteed, and economic conditions can affect both dividend sustainability and capital gains. Ultimately, evaluating the portfolio requires examining each company's financial performance, valuation, dividend sustainability, and exposure to economic risks.
